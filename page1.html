1. Introduction to Statistics

Before diving into complex formulas and engineering applications, it is crucial to understand the foundational terminology and how data is sourced and presented. These are common 2-to-4 mark theory questions in AGE 301 exams.

"Statistics" vs. "Statistic"

It is easy to mix these up, but examiners specifically look for the distinction between the field of study and the numerical measurement.

Statistics (Plural / The Field)

This is the scientific discipline that deals with the collection, organization, analysis, interpretation, and presentation of data. It is the broad subject you are currently studying.

A Statistic (Singular / The Measurement)

This is a single numerical value calculated from a sample. It is used to estimate a characteristic of a whole population (which is called a parameter).

Examples: The sample mean (x̄), the sample standard deviation (s), or a sample variance.

Limitations of Statistics to Note:

  • It deals with aggregates of facts, not isolated individual cases.
  • It is generally only applicable to quantitative data (numbers), not qualitative characteristics (unless quantified).
  • Statistical laws are probabilistic; they are true on average or in the long run, not exact certainties.

Methods of Collecting Data

Data collection is categorized into Primary (data you collect yourself) and Secondary (data collected by someone else).

  • Observation (Primary): Directly observing and recording events as they happen without intervention (e.g., counting the number of defective bolts coming off an assembly line).
  • Experimentation (Primary): Conducting controlled tests to determine cause-and-effect relationships (e.g., the microwave-convective dryer experiment testing banana cultivars).
  • Surveys/Questionnaires (Primary): Gathering data by asking people questions directly (e.g., polling students about a proposed loan scheme).
  • Archival/Documentary (Secondary): Extracting data from existing records, databases, or previous studies (e.g., looking at past machine downtime logs).

Methods of Representing Data

Once data is collected, it must be presented logically. For AGE 301, you must be familiar with constructing these from raw data:

  • Tabular Presentation: Organizing data into rows and columns.
    • Example: Frequency Distribution Tables showing class intervals, midpoints, and frequencies.
  • Graphical Presentation: Visual representation of data to show trends and distributions.
    • Histograms: Bars showing frequency of continuous data class intervals (no gaps between bars).
    • Bar Charts: Bars showing frequencies of discrete categories (gaps between bars).
    • Pie Charts: Circular charts divided into sectors based on relative frequency or degrees (Frequency / Total × 360°).
    • Ogives: Smoothed cumulative frequency polygons used to estimate medians and quartiles.
    • Scatter Diagrams: Plotting points on an X-Y axis to observe correlations.
  • Textual Presentation: Summarizing the data findings in written paragraphs.
